PUBLIC REFERENCE: GARMIN WEARABLE METRICS AND EMA DESIGN

NOTE: This is a sample document for the GLOSS study-documents search. It
summarizes publicly available information from Garmin's owner's manuals and
published research on ecological momentary assessment (EMA). It is secondary
background, not a study protocol, and it is not official guidance from Garmin
or the authors cited. Check the original sources before relying on it.


PART 1: GARMIN WEARABLE METRICS

1.1 Heart rate variability and stress level
Garmin watches estimate stress from heart rate variability (HRV), the
variation in time between heartbeats. The heartbeat is regulated by the
autonomic nervous system. Stress, like exercise, raises heart rate and lowers
HRV. When a person is relaxed and resting, heart rate goes down and HRV goes
up. Low HRV therefore suggests stress (the sympathetic nervous system is
dominant), and high HRV suggests rest (the parasympathetic system is
dominant).

The watch analyzes HRV while the wearer is inactive to determine an overall
stress level from 0 to 100:
- 0-25: resting state
- 26-50: low stress
- 51-75: medium stress
- 76-100: high stress

Training, physical activity, sleep, nutrition and general life stress all
affect stress readings. Garmin recommends wearing the watch while sleeping
for the best results.

What this means for researchers: stress scores are only produced during
inactive periods, so gaps during exercise or walking are expected and are not
necessarily missing data.

1.2 Body Battery
Body Battery is Garmin's estimate of the wearer's energy reserves, described
as working like a gas gauge in a car. The watch combines heart rate
variability, stress level, sleep quality and activity data to calculate it.
The scale runs from 5 to 100:
- 5-25: very low reserve energy
- 26-50: low reserve energy
- 51-75: medium reserve energy
- 76-100: high reserve energy

Good sleep charges Body Battery. Strenuous activity and high stress drain it
more quickly. According to Garmin, food and caffeine do not affect it.
Wearing the watch during sleep makes the estimate more accurate.

1.3 Getting reliable wrist heart rate data
Garmin's manuals give these tips for erratic heart rate data:
- Clean and dry your arm before putting on the watch.
- Avoid sunscreen, lotion and insect repellent under the watch.
- Avoid scratching the heart rate sensor on the back of the watch.
- Wear the watch above the wrist bone, snug but comfortable.
- Wait until the heart rate status icon is solid before starting an activity.
- Warm up for 5 to 10 minutes and get a heart rate reading before starting
  an activity (warm up indoors when it is cold).
- Rinse the watch with fresh water after each workout.

What this means for researchers: many of these tips are worth adding to
participant onboarding materials, since a loose or dirty sensor is a common
cause of poor-quality heart rate and HRV data.


PART 2: DESIGNING ECOLOGICAL MOMENTARY ASSESSMENTS (EMAs)

2.1 What EMA is
Ecological momentary assessment asks participants short questions about
their current state (for example mood, stress or behavior) repeatedly
throughout the day, in their everyday environment, usually through a
smartphone app. Because questions are asked in the moment, EMA reduces recall
bias compared with retrospective questionnaires.

2.2 Key design decisions
When designing an EMA protocol, researchers need to decide:
- How many study days the protocol runs.
- How many prompts to send per day, and whether they are sent at fixed or
  random times (time-contingent), or triggered by an event such as a
  participant reporting a behavior (event-contingent).
- How many questions each survey has and what response scale is used (for
  example a slider or a Likert-type scale).
- How participants are compensated (for example per completed survey, or
  based on the percentage of surveys completed).

The right prompt frequency depends on the research question. States that
change quickly, such as anxiety, may need more frequent prompts than more
stable states. More prompts give richer data but add participant burden.

2.3 What the evidence says about compliance
Compliance means the percentage of scheduled prompts that participants
complete. Many studies aim for about 80%. Dropout means participants who
leave the study early.

- A meta-analysis of 477 articles (496 samples, 677,536 participants in
  total) across many fields of psychology and related disciplines found that
  the average EMA study scheduled 6 assessments per day, lasted 7 days and
  obtained 79% compliance. Studies with more assessments per day tended to
  run for fewer days, but the number of assessments did not predict
  compliance or dropout. Compliance was significantly higher in studies that
  offered financial incentives. Other design and sample characteristics had
  little effect. (Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023)

- A randomized factorial experiment with 411 US adults over 28 days tested
  5 design factors at once: 15 vs. 25 questions per survey, 2 vs. 4 prompts
  per day, $1 per survey vs. percentage-based payment, fixed vs. random
  prompt times, and slider vs. Likert-type response scales. Overall
  compliance was 83.8%, and none of the design factors had a significant
  effect on compliance. Older age, no history of substance use and no current
  depression were associated with higher completion. Higher app usability
  scores were associated with better compliance, and compliance was slightly
  higher on weekdays than weekends and in the evening than the morning.
  (Businelle et al., 2024)

2.4 Practical recommendations
Based on the studies above:
- Within the ranges studied, prompt frequency and survey length can be
  chosen to fit the research question, rather than kept as low as possible
  for the sake of compliance.
- Offer financial incentives. Across hundreds of studies, paying
  participants was linked to higher compliance.
- Invest in app usability. An easy, reliable app was linked to better
  compliance in a controlled experiment.
- Expect lower compliance in some groups (for example younger participants
  or people with current depression) and plan extra support for them.
- Report compliance transparently: explain how compliance was defined,
  report it by study day and wave, and describe whether any factors predicted
  missed prompts.
